\title{Replacing the independent-observation likelihood\\
\large A proposal for the HIKET calibration}
\author{}
\date{Draft, 17 August 2026 --- \emph{proposal, not implemented}}

\maketitle

\begin{keybox}[In one paragraph]
The calibration currently assumes that every soil-carbon measurement is an
independent piece of evidence. It is not: the same plot measured in three different
campaigns gives three strongly related numbers, and plots near each other give
related numbers too. Because of this, the calibration believes it has roughly twice
as much information as it really has, and the excess confidence falls precisely on
the quantity that controls the headline results. This document proposes a specific,
small, closed-form change that fixes the accounting, explains why the obvious
alternative (simply assuming larger measurement error) provably cannot work, and
states honestly what the change will and will not achieve.
\end{keybox}

\section{What the calibration does now}

\subsection{The model prediction}

For each plot $i$ and each measurement occasion $j$, the soil model produces a
predicted carbon stock, which we write $f_{ij}(\theta)$. Here $\theta$ is the full
vector of parameters being calibrated, and the prediction depends on the plot's
litter inputs, its climate, and the year of the measurement. Nothing in this
document changes $f$; the proposal concerns only how predictions are compared with
observations.

\subsection{The current comparison}

Let $y_{ij}$ be the measured stock. The calibration currently assumes
\begin{equation}
\log y_{ij} \;=\; \log f_{ij}(\theta) \;+\; e_{ij},
\qquad
e_{ij} \ \text{independent for every } i \text{ and } j .
\label{eq:before}
\end{equation}
The comparison is made on the logarithmic scale, which means the error is
proportional rather than absolute: an error of ``ten per cent'' means the same
thing on a carbon-poor plot and a carbon-rich one. Each $e_{ij}$ is drawn from a
distribution centred on zero with spread $s$ (currently $0.80$ on the log scale,
optionally with heavier tails).

The word doing the damage in Equation~\ref{eq:before} is \textbf{independent}. In
the code this assumption is not a stated hypothesis; it is the plain summation

\begin{verbatim}
    sum(dnorm(log(meta$soc_obs), mean = log(SOC_hat), sd = s, log = TRUE))
\end{verbatim}

\noindent
(\texttt{calibration\_engine\_transient.R}, likelihood block). Adding log-densities
together \emph{is} the statement that the observations carry separate, non-overlapping
information. With $1205$ observations, the calibration therefore behaves as though it
has $1205$ independent pieces of evidence.

\section{Why that assumption is wrong here}

It is wrong in a way we have measured, not merely suspected.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.6em,style=nextline]
\item[Plots persist.] If a plot's carbon stock is higher than the model expects in
one campaign, it is higher in the next campaign too. The correlation between the
2006 and 2024 measurements of the \emph{same} plot is $0.66$--$0.81$. The formal
summary of this is the \emph{intraclass correlation}, which is simply the share of
the total variation that is attributable to persistent differences between plots
rather than to fluctuation within a plot. Here it is $0.57$--$0.71$: most of the
scatter is a fixed property of the plot.

\item[Neighbours resemble each other.] Grouping plots by latitude band, the band
means vary five to six times more than they would if plots were independent draws
from one national population.

\item[Campaigns differ as blocks.] The three campaigns have different mean offsets
and different spreads, so measurements share whatever was peculiar to their campaign.

\item[A few plots dominate.] The residual distribution has kurtosis close to $7$,
against $3$ for a normal distribution: the tails are far heavier than assumed, so a
small number of plots exert disproportionate influence.
\end{description}

Taken together, the effective number of independent observations is roughly
\textbf{half} the nominal $1205$.

\begin{keybox}[Why this specifically matters]
Every one of these mechanisms acts on the \emph{overall level} of soil carbon rather
than on differences within a plot. The overall level is exactly what pins down the
product of mean residence time and the litter-input multiplier. So the calibration is
most overconfident precisely in the direction that determines the results we report.
An independent symptom: the posterior standard deviation of the input multiplier is
about $0.07$ on the log scale, meaning the calibration claims to know it to within
roughly seven per cent. That claim is not credible given the correlation structure
above, and it is why an externally derived prior, however well founded, currently
changes almost nothing.
\end{keybox}

\section{Why simply assuming a larger error does not work}

The natural first reaction is to keep Equation~\ref{eq:before} and increase $s$. This
provably cannot achieve what is needed, for a reason worth stating carefully.

Increasing $s$ inflates uncertainty in \emph{every} direction of parameter space by
the same factor. But the problem is not that we are uniformly overconfident; we are
overconfident \emph{about the level} while remaining appropriately confident about
within-plot contrasts. To widen the level enough by inflating $s$ alone, $s$ would
have to reach roughly $1.8$, against a measured residual spread of $0.79$. That is
internally contradictory: the error model would assert a scatter more than twice what
the residuals actually show, destroying the self-consistency argument that justifies
the choice of $s$ in the first place.

\begin{keybox}[The one-sentence reason correlation is the right tool]
Introducing correlation between observations widens uncertainty about the level while
leaving the total variance of each individual observation unchanged --- and nothing
else available to us does that.
\end{keybox}

\section{What is proposed}

\subsection{The change}

Add a single term to Equation~\ref{eq:before}:
\begin{equation}
\log y_{ij} \;=\; \log f_{ij}(\theta) \;+\; u_i \;+\; e_{ij},
\qquad
u_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\tau^2),
\qquad
e_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,s^2).
\label{eq:after}
\end{equation}

The new quantity $u_i$ carries no second subscript: there is \emph{one} $u_i$ per
plot, shared by all of that plot's measurements. It represents whatever is
persistently true of that plot and unknown to the model. Because the comparison is
on the log scale, $u_i$ acts multiplicatively: it says ``this plot is persistently
twenty per cent richer than the model believes.'' The parameter $\tau$ controls how
large such plot-level offsets typically are, and $s$ now describes only the
remaining, genuinely occasion-to-occasion variation.

\subsection{This is not an invented convenience}

The interpretation is physical and was identified independently of this proposal.
No model in the ensemble contains any description of local soil conditions --- texture,
drainage, parent material, stoniness beyond the stock correction. Those conditions do
not change between 1989 and 2024. A persistent per-plot offset is therefore exactly
the form the missing information should take. We are writing down a term for something
we already know exists, not adding a free variance to buy comfort.

\subsection{The plot-level offsets are removed analytically, not estimated}

A common objection is that Equation~\ref{eq:after} adds hundreds of unknowns, one per
plot. It does not, because we never estimate them. We integrate them out --- that is,
we ask what the observations look like once every possible value of $u_i$ has been
averaged over, weighted by how plausible it is. This is standard and exact, and the
result has a closed form.

For a plot with $n_i$ measurements, collect its log-residuals
$r_{ij} = \log y_{ij} - \log f_{ij}(\theta)$ into a vector. After integrating out
$u_i$, those residuals are jointly normal with covariance
\begin{equation}
\Sigma_i \;=\; s^2 I \;+\; \tau^2 \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\!\top},
\end{equation}
where $I$ is the identity matrix and $\mathbf{1}$ is a column of ones. Every diagonal
entry is $s^2 + \tau^2$ (each observation's total variance) and every off-diagonal
entry is $\tau^2$ (the covariance any two measurements of the same plot share). This
pattern --- equal variances, equal covariances --- is called \emph{compound symmetry}.

Matrices of this shape can be inverted analytically, so no numerical linear algebra
is required. Writing $n_i$ for the number of observations on plot $i$, the
contribution of that plot to the log-likelihood is
\begin{equation}
\log L_i \;=\; -\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left[
\frac{1}{s^{2}}\!\left(\sum_j r_{ij}^{2}
- \frac{\tau^{2}}{s^{2} + n_i\tau^{2}}\Big(\sum_j r_{ij}\Big)^{\!2}\right)
+ (n_i-1)\log s^{2}
+ \log\!\left(s^{2} + n_i\tau^{2}\right)
+ n_i\log 2\pi
\right].
\label{eq:cs}
\end{equation}

Everything in Equation~\ref{eq:cs} is a sum over the plot's own observations. The cost
is proportional to $n_i$, identical to the current code. Plots remain independent of
one another, so the outer summation over plots is unchanged.

\subsection{Setting $\tau$}

We recommend \textbf{fixing} $\tau$ at the measured intraclass correlation rather than
estimating it. Two reasons. First, it adds no free parameter, so the comparison between
models is not disturbed --- a per-model free variance term would let each model choose
its own tolerance for misfit, reintroducing exactly the asymmetry the homogenised prior
scheme exists to remove. Second, $\tau$ is directly measurable from the residuals
without reference to the calibration, so fixing it is an external constraint rather
than a fitted convenience. The relationship is
\begin{equation}
\rho \;=\; \frac{\tau^{2}}{\tau^{2}+s^{2}},
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\tau^{2} \;=\; \frac{\rho}{1-\rho}\, s^{2},
\end{equation}
with $\rho$ the intraclass correlation, measured at $0.57$--$0.71$. A single common
value across all six models keeps the intercomparison clean; the sensitivity of the
results to $\rho$ within its measured range should be reported.

\section{What changes, concretely}

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.30\linewidth}p{0.31\linewidth}p{0.31\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{Before} & \textbf{After} \\
\midrule
Error structure & one independent draw per plot-year & one draw per plot-year plus one persistent offset per plot \\
Free parameters & unchanged & unchanged ($\tau$ fixed externally) \\
Cost per plot & proportional to $n_i$ & proportional to $n_i$ \\
Code touched & --- & one block in \texttt{calibration\_engine\_transient.R} \\
Variance of a plot's mean & $s^2/n_i$, shrinks toward zero & $s^2/n_i + \tau^2$, does not \\
Confidence in the overall level & too high & appropriately reduced \\
Confidence in within-plot change & appropriate & essentially unchanged \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The fifth row is the mechanism in a single line. At present, averaging more
measurements of a plot drives the uncertainty in that plot's mean toward zero, so
accumulating plots drives uncertainty in the national level toward zero as well. Under
the proposal a floor of $\tau^2$ remains, because additional visits to the same plot
cannot tell us anything new about that plot's persistent peculiarity. Differences
\emph{within} a plot are unaffected, since the shared offset cancels when one
measurement is subtracted from another --- which is why the trend is largely preserved
while the level is properly widened.

\section{What this will and will not achieve}

\begin{warnbox}[State this before running, not after]
The purpose of the change is to make the stated uncertainty honest. It is not
guaranteed to move any particular parameter, and it must not be judged by whether it
does.
\end{warnbox}

A widened level uncertainty allows externally derived priors to carry more weight,
because the relative influence of a prior and a likelihood is set by their relative
precisions. Rough arithmetic suggests that accounting for plot-level correlation alone
would raise the influence of the input-multiplier prior from about seven per cent to
roughly fourteen, and that adding the regional level could bring it near thirty.

\begin{warnbox}[These figures are unreliable and should not be quoted]
They come from a marginal, one-parameter-at-a-time approximation. That same style of
approximation was tested against a full reweighting of the existing posterior draws
and failed badly: once two priors change together, the existing samples provide
almost no coverage of the new region --- an effective sample size of one draw in a
thousand. Direction can be anticipated; magnitude cannot. Only running the calibration
settles it.
\end{warnbox}

\section{Open questions to resolve before implementing}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
\item \textbf{Heavy tails.} The current likelihood optionally uses a Student's
$t$ distribution to accommodate kurtosis near $7$. A $t$ does not decompose into a
shared part plus an independent part the way a normal does, so the two features do
not simply stack. Either apply compound symmetry to the normal version, or adopt a
multivariate $t$ per plot, which preserves closed form but changes the meaning of the
tail parameter. This must be decided deliberately rather than discovered at runtime.

\item \textbf{The regional level.} Plots within a latitude band are also correlated.
This can be added as a second, nested shared term, still in closed form, at the cost
of the outer summation no longer being over independent units. It is the larger of the
two effects on the national level and should probably be included, but it is a bigger
change and could follow as a second step.

\item \textbf{Comparability of log-likelihoods.} Changing the likelihood changes its
normalising constant. Log-likelihoods from before and after are therefore \emph{not}
comparable, and neither are any quantities derived from them. Comparisons across the
change must use quantities computed after the fact, such as root-mean-square error or
the per-draw distributions reported in the appendix on posterior RMSE.

\item \textbf{Campaign effects.} A campaign-specific \emph{spread} is safe and could
be added. A campaign-specific \emph{mean offset} must not be: the trend we are trying
to estimate \emph{is} the difference between campaign levels, so a free offset absorbs
the signal. This has already been demonstrated --- a fitted offset of $-0.137$ implied
that no accumulation had occurred at all.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Recommended sequence}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
\item Measure $\rho$ once more on the current residuals and fix $\tau$ from it.
\item Implement Equation~\ref{eq:cs} for the normal branch only, leaving the Student's
$t$ branch untouched and selectable, so the change is reversible by a switch.
\item Verify on a short run that the log-likelihood is computed correctly, by checking
that setting $\tau=0$ reproduces the current values exactly.
\item Run the full calibration; compare using RMSE distributions and parameter
locations, never log-likelihood.
\item Only then consider the regional level.
\end{enumerate}

Step three is the one that must not be skipped: $\tau = 0$ reducing exactly to the
present likelihood is the only check that distinguishes a correct implementation from
a plausible-looking one.
