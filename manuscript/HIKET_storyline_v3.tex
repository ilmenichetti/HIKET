\subsection{A sink that appears to be levelling off}
\paragraph{What the inventory measures.} On the balanced plot set the mean stock runs
$64.5 \to 71.5 \to 73.6$~tC\,ha$^{-1}$ across the three campaigns: $+7.0$, then $+2.1$.
Accumulation, bending over. \FIG{F3_mean_SOCalt}
\begin{note}
\textbf{Opening figure, deliberately model-free (2026-09-04).} v3 opened on
\textsf{F3\_mean\_soc}, which draws six model trajectories through the campaign means --- a Results
answer shown before the question. \textsf{F3\_mean\_SOCalt} is the same data on the same basis with
the models removed: three campaign means, the two segments between them, and their slopes. The model
version now appears where it belongs, at the first Results claim.
\end{note}

\paragraph{The rate, and what it is a rate of.} $+0.259 \pm 0.040$~tC\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ over
1985--2024 on the balanced set, whole profile, true observation years. Over the last interval alone
it is $+0.117 \pm 0.066$ --- a confidence interval that includes zero.
\begin{warn}
\textbf{Which of these two numbers carries which claim --- state it, and never let them swap.} The
\textbf{full span} ($+0.259$) is what the models are judged against, and it is the robust one: it
survives a shared campaign-level offset of up to $\sim$9\%. The \textbf{last interval} ($+0.117$) is
fragile --- it dissolves at $\tau_C\approx2\%$, and no field replication exists to measure that
offset. \ \raisebox{0pt}{$\bigstar$}~\textbf{The uncomfortable part:} the levelling-off, which is the
paper's opening hook, is the \emph{contrast} between the two intervals --- so the hook rests partly on
the fragile number even though the model comparison rests on the robust one. Say so where the hook is
introduced, not only in the limitations. Memory \texttt{campaign-comparability-limits-the-trend}.
\end{warn}
\begin{ann}
\textbf{Annotation (p3): ``Which rate? And which report?''} \textbf{Fixed here:} the basis is now
stated wherever a rate appears (balanced set $n=310$, unweighted, whole profile, true years), because
the same data give $+0.209$ on the pairwise set --- a factor $1.8$ on the headline. The reporting
basis was decided on 2026-08-17 and every quoted rate in the paper must name it.
\end{ann}

\paragraph{Why the levelling-off is the interesting part.} A soil that is still accumulating and a
soil that has stopped are the same stock and different futures. The second derivative, not the
stock, is what an inventory projection turns on.

\begin{warn}
\textbf{Do not present the observed rate as truth} (annotation, p7). Three campaigns measured by
three protocols over four decades give a rate with a real and partly unquantified comparability
error: 1985 is a decade wide, its organic layer had to be reconstructed, and the short interval's
CI includes zero. The paper states what was measured and how, and treats agreement between models
and observations as consistency rather than as validation.
\end{warn}

\subsection{Why it matters for the national greenhouse gas balance}
\paragraph{The soil term is produced by a model, not measured.} Finland reports forest mineral-soil
carbon with a Tier~3 method: inventory data and biomass models supply a litter input, Yasso07 converts
it to an annual stock change for dead organic matter and soil organic matter combined, to one metre,
in two regions. No soil flux is measured anywhere in the chain.
\refs{\vd~Statistics Finland NID 2024 \S6.4 (method in force; \emph{``builds on the research by Liski
et al.~(2006)''}); \vd~Liski et al.~2006 (the method's founding paper)}

\paragraph{And it is large enough to decide the sign.} In 2022 mineral forest soils were reported as a
sink of $4.8$~Mt~CO$_2$ beside a living-biomass sink of $12.6$, while the forest land sink had fallen
$84\%$ since 1990 and the LULUCF sector had become a net source of $4.4$~Mt~CO$_2$\,eq. The soil term
carries $31.5\%$ uncertainty against $16.3\%$ for tree biomass, and Forest Land's combined figure is
$83.9\%$.
\refs{\vd~NID 2024 \S6.1, \S6.4.3 (all figures); \vd~Lehtonen \& Heikkinen 2015 (source of the
$31.5\%$ --- and of our $\sinput$ prior centre, which is worth saying once)}

\paragraph{\raisebox{0pt}{$\bigstar$} The chain states its own initialisation assumption, and that is
the proposition this paper tests.} The inventory fixes the initial soil state by a spin-up to steady
state --- on NFI6 (1971--76) litter and 1960--90 mean weather --- justified in one sentence:
\emph{``approximately ten years of simulation since spin-up is enough to cancel out the effect of the
spin-up level''}. We test that over a $35$-year observed window with the initial state calibrated
rather than assumed.
\refs{\vd~NID 2024 \S6.4 (verbatim); \nd~Peltoniemi et al.~2006, For.~Ecol.~Manage.~232:75--85 --- the
paper it cites, \textbf{read before quoting}}
\begin{warn}
\textbf{Tone.} Quote the \emph{inventory} citing Peltoniemi et al., not Peltoniemi et al.\ directly:
the 2006 claim is probably narrower than the use made of it, and Mikko is a close collaborator. The
sentence is a foil for a \emph{convention}, never for a colleague.
\end{warn}

\paragraph{What the inventory asks a model to do.} Not to rank plots, but to reproduce a national
trajectory over decades and project it forward. That is the quantity this paper evaluates, and
saying so early prevents a later result (near-zero plot-level skill) from reading as failure.

\subsection{The convention: soils initialised at steady state}
\paragraph{It is the standard across the lineages used for forest soil reporting.} Yasso in the
Finnish inventory (10\,000-year run to steady state), RothC --- where the equilibrium run additionally
back-solves the annual carbon input from the measured stock --- and the spin-ups of global land
models. The instructive exception is Canada's CBM-CFS3, which initialises through repeated rotations
of growth and disturbance and so reports a deliberately non-equilibrium state.
\refs{\vd~Lehtonen et al.~2016 (\emph{``running \dots\ into a steady state''}, and its premise:
\emph{``if we assume that this average level of inputs and climate has remained steady over
centuries''}); \vd~NID 2024 \S6.4; \vd~Contreras et al.~2026 (RothC practice); \nd~Kurz et al.~2009
(CBM-CFS3, secondary so far); \nd~Parton et al.~1987 (Century)}

\paragraph{\raisebox{0pt}{$\bigstar$} And Liski et al.~(2006) is the antecedent: they ran the
transient simulation and \emph{assumed} the state.} Same country, same model family, same
inventory-derived litter, the same idea of following the management history forward ---
\emph{``the soil and litter carbon pools at the beginning of the study period were calculated by
assuming a steady state \dots\ starting from this steady state in 1922, the model was run using
annually varying values of litter input and temperature.''} This paper keeps the run and
\textbf{calibrates the state}. One assumption apart, and it is the one repeated campaigns can inform.
\refs{\vd~Liski et al.~2006, Ann.~For.~Sci.~63:687--697 (verbatim, \S2)}
\begin{note}
\textbf{Lineage, not correction} (Lorenzo, 2026-09-04: \emph{``that's the most important
reference''}). Aleksi and Mikko are coauthors on it. The paper evolves the group's own method; frame
it collegially throughout, and give Liski the position in the Introduction that this draft previously
gave to a passing $\sinput$ comparison.
\end{note}

\paragraph{The convention has been criticised before --- what has been missing is the test.} The
equilibrium assumption is known to bias the calibration, not only the initial state: fitting to
current stocks under it overestimates the decay rate of the slowest pool, and spin-up overestimates
recently disturbed sites. Where it has been relaxed, the replacement state has been \emph{imposed} ---
from an assumed disturbance history, a thermal proxy, or a prescribed correction --- rather than
inferred from observed change in the stock.
\refs{\vd~Wutzler \& Reichstein 2007, Biogeosciences 4:125--136 (with Yasso; small accumulation makes
implied equilibrium stocks \emph{``virtually approach infinity''}); \vd~Carvalhais et al.~2008
(relaxing it: model efficiency $+21\%$, biased parameters removed); \vd~Kanari et al.~2022;
\vd~Lee \& Viscarra Rossel 2020 ($-2.0$ to $-4.5$~Mg\,C\,ha$^{-1}$ from initialisation alone);
\vd~Le No\"e et al.~2023 (\raisebox{0pt}{$\sim$}250 models, little independent time-series validation)}
\begin{warn}
\textbf{``Near-universal and rarely examined'' has been cut, and must not come back.} It is
checkable and wrong: Wutzler \& Reichstein made essentially this argument in 2007, using Yasso. The
defensible novelty is narrower and still a first --- \textbf{the initial state calibrated against
repeated national soil inventories}, which is what the campaigns now allow.
\end{warn}

\paragraph{What steady state assumes.} In all of these models a pool loses carbon in proportion to
how much it holds, and nothing else. Under a constant input and a constant climate such a system
drifts to one stock and stays there: input multiplied by mean residence time, the familiar
$C^{*}=J/k$. Starting a simulation at that stock is not an assumption about the soil. It is an
assumption about its \emph{history} --- that input and climate have been effectively unchanged for
long enough that even the slowest pool has caught up, and that nothing has disturbed it in between.
\begin{ann}
\textbf{Annotation (p6): ``\emph{`donor-controlled' sounds jargon in this context, be simpler.''}}
\textbf{Done} --- the property is now stated in words (``a pool loses carbon in proportion to how much
it holds''). The term itself belongs in the Methods, once, if at all.
\end{ann}
\refs{\vd~Olson 1963, Ecology 44:322--331 ($X=L/k$); \nd~\AA gren \& Bosatta 1998 \emph{or}
Manzoni \& Porporato 2009 (general form --- pick one); \nd~Sierra et al.~2017 (age/turnover/transit
vocabulary, to keep our terms exact)}

\paragraph{Two properties follow, and the paper turns on both.} The equilibrium condition fixes only
the \emph{product} $J\times\tau$, so a level alone can never separate a fast soil fed much litter from
a slow soil fed little --- \textbf{the ridge is derivable from the model definition, before any
sampler runs}. And the stationary state is a function of the parameters alone, so an equilibrium start
has no distance left to travel: it can track a moving target, but it cannot express a soil still
catching up.
\refs{\vd~derivation, ours (\texttt{literature/03\_equilibrium\_theory}); \nd~Luo et al.~2017,
Biogeosciences 14:145--161 (storage \emph{capacity} vs \emph{potential} --- adopt the vocabulary only
after checking their definitions)}

\paragraph{And what follows from it.} An equilibrium start is already at the end of the accumulation.
It cannot produce a rise, so it cannot show a rise levelling off; the very dynamic the inventory
needs is the one the convention defines away.

\subsection{Finnish forests are a managed landscape, not an equilibrium one}
\paragraph{The history is the argument.} Slash-and-burn across most of the productive forest land of
the east, woodland grazing suppressing regeneration, tar burning and high-grading: by the first
inventory, old trees were effectively absent from southern Finland outside the least accessible
areas and forest volume was at its historical low. Then a century of managed regrowth --- growing
stock from roughly $1.4$ to $2.6$ billion~m$^3$, $+84\%$, most of it after the late 1960s.
\FIG{F10b_forest_history}
\refs{\vd~Aakala, Kulha \& Kuuluvainen 2023, Landscape Ecol.~38:2417--2431 (\emph{``in eastern Finland
almost all productive forest land was used in slash-and-burn cultivation''}; population density the
strongest predictor of depletion); \vd~Korhonen et al.~2024 (the growing-stock series)}

\paragraph{The managed landscape still sits below its own unmanaged counterparts.} Old unmanaged
stands hold about $40\%$ more carbon in the mineral soil than managed stands of comparable site types
--- and their own stocks do not change over a twenty-year resampling.
\refs{\vd~Kumpu, Peltoniemi, Forsius \& M\"akel\"a 2025, Silva Fennica 59:24072 (0--10~cm $2.44$ vs
$1.78$, 10--20~cm $1.62$ vs $1.14$~kg\,C\,m$^{-2}$, both $p<0.001$; resampled change $-692$~kg\,C\,ha$^{-1}$,
$p=0.75$); \nd~comparability of their depth basis with our whole-profile target}

\paragraph{\raisebox{0pt}{$\bigstar$} And the lag is long enough to matter --- stated by the
antecedent itself.} Even with litter held at its 2004 level, Liski et al.\ found carbon would keep
accumulating and the stocks would settle \emph{``centuries later \dots\ at a $38\%$ higher level than
in 1922''}. Peltoniemi et al.\ had already attributed part of the soil carbon of Finnish plots to a
slash-and-burn history ending in the early twentieth century, and concluded that the long-term trend
\emph{``cannot be distinguished from the measured data unless the measurements cover at least two
rotations''}. Three campaigns over four decades is what makes the state estimable.
\refs{\vd~Liski et al.~2006 \S4 (verbatim); \vd~Peltoniemi et al.~2004, GCB 10:2078--2091 (verbatim);
\vd~Wutzler \& Reichstein 2007 (soils disturbed \emph{centuries} ago remain transient)}

\dparagraph{\raisebox{0pt}{$\bigstar$} And our own posteriors say the same thing, which is the
reconciliation this section needs.} Holding litter at its present level and letting each calibrated
model run to its own equilibrium leaves a headroom of $+28$ to $+49\%$ above the modelled 1985 stock
in five of the six --- Yasso07 $+44$, Yasso15 $+47$, Yasso20 $+28$, TP2 $+49$, TP3 $+82$, with SP1 the
outlier at $+5$. Liski et al.\ put it at $+38\%$. \textbf{Two independent routes --- their
equilibrium-started forward run, our calibrated transient posterior --- land on the same
disequilibrium.}
\refs{ours, \texttt{doublechecks/equilibrium\_headroom.R} (arm B, 4 draws/model)}
\begin{ann}
\textbf{Annotation (p7): ``\emph{This need to be reconciled with our results with a discussion.}''}
\textbf{Done, and it corroborates rather than conflicts.} \textbf{Note the bases differ} --- Liski's
$38\%$ is relative to 1922, ours to the modelled 1985 state --- so the agreement is in magnitude and
direction, not decimal for decimal. SP1's $+5\%$ is the informative exception: a single slow pool is
already close to its own equilibrium, which is why it is the model that cannot produce the observed
accumulation.
\end{ann}
\begin{warn}
\textbf{Consistency check with Strand 2.} Liski et al.\ describe continued accumulation under litter
frozen at 2004. Our forward arm freezes inputs at 2024 and finds the same thing --- so \textbf{cite
them there}, and do not present it as new. See memory \texttt{forecast-sink-robustness}.
\end{warn}
\begin{test}
\textbf{\nd~The check that would make this section decisive.} Liski et al.\ report soil carbon going
$6.1\to6.3$~kg\,C\,m$^{-2}$ over 1922--2004: about $+0.024$~tC\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$, an order of
magnitude below the $+0.259$ our campaigns show for 1985--2024. A model started full has little room
to accumulate --- the thesis of this paper, in the antecedent's own numbers. \textbf{Verify first}:
their figure is a national mean over a \emph{changing} forest area (the denominator implies
$\sim$14~Mha), on a ``soil and litter'' basis, over a different period.
\end{test}

\subsection{Objectives}
\paragraph{Why the method and the result are the same thing here.} The convention is the reason the
national trajectory has never been tested: a soil started at equilibrium reproduces a stock but has no
trajectory to be wrong about, so the question of whether these models can follow four decades of
change has not been asked of them. Replacing the assumption is therefore not a methodological preface
to the results --- it is what makes the results exist. This is stated once, here, so that a reader
does not arrive at the Results expecting a methods paper.
\begin{note}
\textbf{Why this paragraph is here.} The Introduction builds on the initialisation convention, which
promises a \emph{methods} contribution; the weight budget puts $\sim$55\% on the \emph{national
trend}, an empirical one. Without the joint stated explicitly, the paper reads as changing its mind
at the section break.
\end{note}

\paragraph{Three questions, in the order the paper weights them.}
(i) Once the starting state is estimated from the measured trajectory, where does that trajectory
place the models on the input/turnover exchange, relative to their published parameterisations, and
how far does it narrow that exchange? (Reaching the stocks and the rate is a precondition, reported as
such.) (ii) Does the position on the exchange reach the extrapolation: do two calibrations that
reproduce the same window from different positions project differently, and for which structures?
(iii) What is left that none of the models can see, and what would it take to see it?
\begin{note}
\textbf{Reworded 2026-09-16.} (i) used to lead with ``does a transient start reproduce the
trajectory?'' --- a yes/no the Results then discount as consistency, not validation. The title says
\emph{displaces}; the question now asks \emph{where}. Behind it sit the two advancements as Lorenzo
put them: the state is \emph{estimated} (the trajectory informs it) and its date is \emph{pushed back}
to 1917 (the error in assuming balance decays through 68 years of pre-run; what does not decay, the
estimate takes up). (ii) stays on extrapolation in the Introduction; warming is named only in Strand 2.
\end{note}

\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Data}
\paragraph{Plots and campaigns.} Finnish NFI/BioSoil/Komeetta permanent plots; three SOC campaigns
(1985--95, 2006, 2024); 456 calibration-ready plots, 1205 plot-year observations.

\paragraph{One homogenized SOC basis.} Organic plus mineral to a per-plot variable depth cap plus a
modelled deep tail, reproducing the official national stocks exactly. \FIG{F2_baseline}

\paragraph{Litter input.} The Tupek product, tree litter including harvest residues and natural
mortality, excluding understorey. \FIG{F1_inputs}

\subsection{The model ladder}
\paragraph{One operational model, and three benchmarks to check it against.} The object of study is
Yasso --- it is what the inventory runs, in three published versions. SP1, TP2 and TP3 are added as a
one-, two- and three-pool benchmark ladder whose behaviour is transparent, so that any result can be
read against a structure simple enough to reason about by hand. \textbf{They are a control, not
competitors}: the question they answer is whether a finding is a property of the data and the
transient design, or an artefact of a particular five-pool implementation.
\begin{warn}
\textbf{The word ``ensemble'' has to be handled carefully, and this draft was not handling it.} Six
models run together is not an ensemble in the sense the phrase carries in this literature --- a set
sampled to span structural uncertainty, whose spread is a product. Ours is one operational model plus
a benchmark ladder, and the spread between them is a \emph{diagnostic}, not an uncertainty estimate.
Say ``all six models'' where the meaning is ``all of them''; keep ``ensemble'' for the gridded maps,
where averaging the six genuinely is what is being delivered.
\end{warn}
\begin{ann}
\textbf{Annotation (p9):} ``\emph{we are not really doing any structural uncertainty work \dots\ We
have the simpler structures as a benchmark, mainly, with Yasso as the proven standard.}''
\textbf{Adopted:} the ladder is described as a benchmark design, and the word ``structural
uncertainty'' is used only where it is earned (the forward spread), never as a product claim.
\end{ann}

\subsection{Transient initialisation}
\paragraph{The start becomes a parameter.} Each draw spins up from 1917 under a reconstructed input
ramp and is carried forward through all three campaigns; $\sinit$ scales the 1917 input level.
\FIG{F4_initialization}

\subsection{A fair frame}
\paragraph{Equal information, not equal parameter count.} Yasso does not fit its decomposition rates,
so the simple models do not either: intrinsic rates are externally anchored (ICBM), humification
fractions are free, and every model calibrates the same auxiliary pair.

\subsection{Calibration and error model}
\paragraph{One likelihood, and it does not pretend plots are independent.} Log-normal, with region,
plot and campaign offsets marginalised at fixed variances.

\subsection{Analyses}
\paragraph{Three, matching the three objectives.} Posterior predictive over the observed window; a
forward experiment contrasting published and calibrated parameters under climate scenarios; and a
residual analysis against site covariates.

\subsubsection{The models reach the observed levels}
\paragraph{No model sits more than $3.5$~tC\,ha$^{-1}$ from any campaign mean.} On the current
calibration all six models reproduce all three campaign levels. \FIG{F3_mean_soc}

\dparagraph{Reaching those levels is not the free parameter it would be in a conventional
calibration.} The equilibrium stock is $J\times\tau$, and if both factors were free, hitting a level
would be arithmetic rather than a result. Neither is free here. The decomposition rates are
externally anchored and not fitted --- Yasso's $a$-vector is a fixed constant, the simple models'
fast rate likewise and their slow rate pinned at the ICBM value --- and the input multiplier is held
to a prior of log-SD $0.25$ around an independently derived centre. What the six models are asked to
do is reach \emph{three} campaign levels, decades apart, on anchored kinetics and a constrained
input, from a start that is itself inferred. That they all do is the evidence that the fair frame
works.

\dparagraph{What it cannot do is discriminate.} Every point on the input/turnover ridge reaches the
same level by construction, so agreement here separates neither the six structures from one another
nor our calibration from the published one. It is the precondition for the two claims that follow ---
the rate and the shape --- and it is read as such, not as support for them.

\subsubsection{Structure is nearly invisible in fit}

\dparagraph{So fit cannot arbitrate between these structures.} Whatever separates them, it is not
the calibration data --- which is why the benchmark ladder is read as a control on the operational
model rather than as a competition, and why Strand 2 has to ask the question somewhere else: in
extrapolation, where the structures do separate.

\subsubsection{And plot by plot there is no gradient to fit}
\paragraph{The models impose an ordering across plots; the observations do not have one.} Holdout
$R^2$ is zero to three decimals in all six, and the fitted line through predicted-against-observed is
flat. But the informative part of the figure is not the flatness --- it is that the \emph{predicted}
spread is the wider of the two ($0$--$270$ against an observed $30$--$220$), and that it is cleanly
ordered by stand basal area, the models' only real between-plot driver, while the observations show no
such ordering. \FIG{F6_obs_vs_pred_holdout}

\dparagraph{So the problem is not that the models are uninformative about plots --- it is that they
are confidently wrong about them.} Litter input varies across plots and the models translate that
faithfully into carbon; observed SOC is ordered by something else. Between-plot variation in this
landscape is dominated by local factors --- edaphic, hydrological, historical --- for which no model
here has a term, and against which a litter-driven gradient is not merely weak but misdirected.

\dparagraph{An ordering the models do not carry is a question, and it is the last strand's.} If the
observations are ordered by something absent from every model here, the question is what --- and that question is answerable with
data already in hand (Strand 3).
\paragraph{\raisebox{0pt}{$\bigstar$} Measured: the models express a between-plot gradient an order
of magnitude steeper than the soil's.} Regressing $\log$~SOC on stand basal area gives a model
response of
$+0.044$ to $+0.049$ per m$^2$\,ha$^{-1}$ ($r=0.62$--$0.73$) against an observed $+0.0044$
($r=0.11$). The residual therefore falls with basal area in \textbf{all six models}, at
$-0.032$ to $-0.044$, with $p$ from $10^{-16}$ to $10^{-111}$ and on holdout, calibration and pooled
data alike. Across the observed range of $40$~m$^2$\,ha$^{-1}$ the models make soil carbon span a
factor of $\mathbf{6.3}$; the observations span $\mathbf{1.19}$.
\refs{ours, \texttt{doublechecks/basal\_area\_sign.R} (arm B run; residual basis
$\log\mathrm{obs}-\log\mathrm{pred}$, identical to the RF target)}

\dparagraph{And the little that the data do show is mostly geography.} Controlling for region and soil
type, the observed slope falls from $0.00438$ to $0.00184$ and stops being significant ($p=0.12$),
while the model slope is unmoved at $0.0444$. On that adjusted basis the mismatch is a factor of
$\mathbf{24}$, and the observed span across the whole basal-area range is $1.08$ --- statistically
indistinguishable from no dependence at all.
\paragraph{Where the gradient comes from: the litter product, transmitted faithfully.} The models
carry no basal-area term. They carry litter input, and matched in time to the same 1985 basal area
that gradient is $d\log J/d\mathrm{BA} = +0.072$ --- a span of $\times 18$ across the observed range.
The modelled stock's own slope is $+0.073$ to $+0.076$. \textbf{The models pass the input gradient
through essentially one-for-one}, and the forward run then damps it (the predictive slope is
$+0.044$ to $+0.049$). There is no amplification to explain and no defect to find.
\refs{ours, \texttt{doublechecks/basal\_area\_sign.R} and \texttt{woody\_compartment\_test.R}}

\begin{test}
\textbf{\vd~Tested and refuted 2026-09-04: it is not a compartment mismatch.} The hypothesis was that
Yasso's stock includes woody debris a soil core cannot contain, so the mismatch would grow with
standing biomass. Zeroing the woody litter inputs moves the gradient by $6\%$ (Yasso15
$0.0755 \to 0.0710$) against a $15\times$ gap to the observations. \textbf{And the decisive control is
free:} SP1, TP2 and TP3 carry a single scalar litter flux with \emph{no} composition information at
all, and they produce the \emph{same} slope ($0.073$). A mechanism absent from three of the six
cannot explain a gradient present in all six.
\end{test}

\dparagraph{So the finding belongs to the input product and to the soil, not to the models.} The
litter product says a plot's carbon input varies eighteen-fold across the basal-area range; the soil
says its carbon varies by a factor of $1.2$. Two things follow, and the second is the more useful.
\textbf{(i)} Plot-level soil carbon is not set by current litter input --- each plot sits at a
different point on its own disturbance trajectory, and one global initial state cannot express that.
\textbf{(ii)} \textbf{An eighteen-fold input gradient is not credible on its face.} A stand-keyed
product gives a freshly cut plot almost no litter, and a freshly cut plot does not stop receiving
carbon: ground vegetation, dying roots and residues continue. The plot-scale failure is therefore
evidence for the \emph{same} missing flux as \S\ref{sec:missingflux}, reached from the opposite end
--- and it points at the two components that section is weakest on, understorey and early-succession
vegetation, because those are exactly where a low-basal-area plot's input has gone.

\subsubsection{The data pin a level, and little else}
\paragraph{Convergence is not identifiability.} R-hat $\le 1.008$ everywhere, yet the transfer
fractions are prior-pinned, ridge-correlated and carry under a nat.
\FIG{F8b_fraction_ridges}

\dparagraph{Clean convergence on a non-identified posterior is the expected result of fixing the
mixing, not a failure to notice a problem.} Clean R-hat with prior-pinned marginals, near-perfect anti-correlation between
fractions leaving the same pool, and sub-nat information gain are three independent signatures of the
same thing: the data constrain the net flux out of a pool, never the split. It also sets the limit of
what follows --- what the campaigns identify is a level and a rate, which is what the inventory needs
and is all the paper claims.

\subsubsection{What might be missing from the input flux}\label{sec:missingflux}
\begin{ann}
\textbf{Annotation (p12), the longest one:} ``\emph{The input functions have a lot of trust in the
field \dots\ they do work (I think because the previous fitting compensates) \dots\ there needs to be
a very extensive review of the supporting evidence, with hypotheses well grounded in literature about
what we might be missing in the fluxes (first things to examine are fine root MRT, exudates and
mycorrhizae) \dots\ also missing pioneer vegetation.}'' \textbf{This section is the answer to that
note, and it is the main place where the paper stops being about models.}
\end{ann}

\paragraph{The candidate is not that the litter model is wrong, but that it is incomplete.} Litter
input products are trusted because they have worked for decades --- and one reason they work is that
a calibration free to adjust rates can absorb a missing flux without visible misfit. A transient
calibration on a fixed rate anchor removes that compensation, and what is missing then has to appear
somewhere.

\paragraph{The size of what has to be explained.} Effective flux $3.19$--$4.34$ against a supplied
$\bar J=2.511$~tC\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$: a gap of $\mathbf{0.68}$--$\mathbf{1.83}$. The candidates
below are ordered by how well the literature prices them, \emph{not} by how well they help.
\refs{ours, arm B run \texttt{20260831\_1624*}; \vd~Liski et al.~2006 implies $\sinput\approx1.27$
independently --- the floor of our range}

\paragraph{Rhizodeposition --- the one with an accounting precedent.} Cropland carbon accounting has
carried this term for two decades: the standard approach adds an ``extra-root material'' pool at
$65\%$ of standing root biomass. Forest inventory chains add nothing analogous. And root-derived
carbon is not a low-efficiency input --- at Ultuna its optimised humification coefficient is about
$2.3\times$ that of the same mass of aboveground residues.
\refs{\vd~Bolinder et al.~2007, Agric.~Ecosyst.~Environ.~118:29--42 (the $\times1.65$; RD coefficients
in the literature span $0.5$--$2.0$); \vd~K\"atterer, Bolinder, Andr\'en, Kirchmann \& Menichetti 2011,
AEE 141:184--192 (the $2.3\times$); \nd~a boreal-forest rhizodeposition estimate}
\begin{warn}
\textbf{The cropland factor cannot be carried over at face value.} In cropland, root biomass is a
harvest-time \emph{snapshot}, so the $65\%$ compensates for within-season turnover \emph{and} root
hairs \emph{and} exudates. A forest chain already counts fine-root turnover explicitly (at
$0.85$~yr$^{-1}$), so the forest analogue covers only what is left and \textbf{must be smaller}. The
claim is therefore: cropland accounting recognises a belowground input beyond root biomass and prices
it; forest accounting recognises none; the truth is in between.
\ \vd~\textbf{Answered 2026-09-04, and it weakens this objection.} Bolinder et al.\ derive the $0.65$
as $33/50$ --- the share of belowground C \emph{released by living roots and remaining in soil} over
the share \emph{remaining in roots} --- and state that the extra-root pool is
\emph{``exudates, as well as root hairs and fine roots sloughed off during the growing season which,
because of sampling difficulties, are not included in the `root' fraction.''} That is not the
within-season turnover a forest chain already counts; and forest fine-root biomass is estimated from
cores and root:leaf allometry, which has the \emph{same} sampling problem. \ \nd~a boreal tracer
estimate of the same ratio; \nd~whether our chain's fine-root biomass misses sloughed material.
\end{warn}

\paragraph{Understorey --- outside the litter product by construction, though not outside the
inventory.} The national inventory carries a ground-vegetation litter term; the litter product used
here is a tree-litter product and does not, by design. The understorey share also rises strongly
northward, so a single national multiplier is wrong in opposite directions at the two ends of the
country. \FIG{SX_input_vs_literature}
\begin{ann}

\subsection*{\textsc{Strand 2 --- does the starting-state assumption reach the warming response?} \normalfont(\emph{$\sim$25\%})}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Strand 2 --- does the starting-state assumption reach the warming response? (~25\%)}

\begin{ann}
\textbf{Annotation (p17): ``For what? Do you mean if it has consequences for extrapolation, for
example?''} \textbf{Yes --- and the section is now titled that way.} v2's ``does the ridge position
matter?'' left the reader to guess the predicate. It matters \emph{for extrapolation}: two
calibrations that fit the observed window identically can project differently.
\end{ann}

\begin{note}
\textbf{The link that was missing (2026-09-16).} The strand used to ask whether ridge position
matters, without saying \emph{why we are at this position}. We are here because the start was
estimated: all six models put the product $J\times\tau$ $28$--$49\%$ above the observed stock (the
headroom), whereas a balanced start sets that product \emph{equal} to the stock. A balanced start with
free rates therefore lands at a smaller product --- faster turnover, or less input --- and stores the
missing history in the rates (Wutzler \& Reichstein 2007). In a single-modifier structure the rates
\emph{are} the temperature sensitivity, so the initialisation assumption reaches the warming response
through them. Inductively, the direction for Yasso07 is a \emph{steeper} response than ours, and ours
($\xi$ $\times 2.13$, $\beta_1$ $0.099 \to 0.158$) is already steeper than published. \textbf{Tested:}
two positions on the ridge, F15. \textbf{Not tested:} the balanced-start arm itself
(\texttt{NEXT\_RUN\_equilibrium\_init.md}) --- the initialisation $\to$ position step is deductive.
\end{note}

\subsubsection{The question the ridge forces}
\paragraph{The data cannot choose a point on the ridge, so the choice must be judged by its
consequences.} If moving along it changes nothing we can observe, the displacement is bookkeeping.
If it changes the projection, it is a result with consequences for anyone using these models.

\subsubsection{The test: published parameters against ours, forward only}
\paragraph{No re-calibration, one input parameter refitted.} Both arms are pushed through the same
machinery under ramped scenarios; both match the observed window at least as well as each other, so
any divergence afterwards is extrapolation. \FIG{F15_forward_arms}

\subsubsection{It matters for one structure and not the others}
\paragraph{Only Yasso07 separates.} $-2.08$ $[-3.57, -0.79]$~tC\,ha$^{-1}$ at SSP2-4.5; Yasso15 and
Yasso20 do not separate at all.

\dparagraph{And the mechanism is structural.} Yasso07 applies a single climate modifier to every pool,
so turnover and temperature sensitivity are tied to the same parameter and moving along the ridge
moves both. Yasso15 and Yasso20 carry pool-specific modifiers, and the pools that \emph{hold} the
carbon respond almost identically in both arms. \FIG{A1_forward_appendix}

\dparagraph{The framing that survives.} Yasso15 and Yasso20 are not shown to be right; they are shown
to be \textbf{resilient} to an input assumption the data cannot settle. That is a usable statement
for an inventory, and a narrower one than ``our calibration changes the forecast''.

\begin{warn}
\textbf{The main forecast contains no climate change, and the paper must say so.} The projection
recycles the last twenty observed years and freezes litter at 2024, so its sink is relaxation toward
equilibrium, not a response. The scenarios appear only in this side arc, as an instrument for testing
ridge position. Doing it properly would need productivity to respond to warming as well.
\end{warn}

\subsubsection{The models order the national trajectory but not the plots}
\paragraph{Two statements that read as a contradiction only if separated.} All six models reproduce
the national mean trajectory and explain almost none of the between-plot variance. Both are true,
and the second is a statement about spatial resolution, not about the trajectory.

\subsubsection{What is left behind is recoverable, not irreducible}
\paragraph{Roughly half of the residual variance is predictable from covariates already in hand.} A
random forest on site variables explains $44$--$65\%$ of what the models leave (out-of-bag), with
observation reliability around $0.49$ --- so this is not a noise ceiling. \FIG{F11_rf_heatmap}

\paragraph{The same covariates lead in all six models.} Stand basal area first, then development
class and coarse-fragment content; the importance is diffuse rather than concentrated in one term.

\subsubsection{What that suggests, briefly}
\dparagraph{Two of the leaders are productivity proxies and one is soil-physical.} A productivity
proxy leading everywhere is what a spatially varying input level would look like; a soil-physical
variable leading is something none of the six has a term for. \textbf{We name the
observation and stop there} --- the method that would exploit it is not this paper's to choose.

\section{Conclusions}
\begin{ann}
\textbf{Annotation (p22): ``The policy deliverable should not be a real conclusion, it is more a nice
bonus product. The conclusions \dots\ are more about the opening stories, their resolutions.''}
\textbf{Rebuilt exactly that way:} the conclusions below answer the three opening questions in the
order of the weight budget, and the deliverable is demoted to a closing paragraph.
\end{ann}

\paragraph{A transient, calibrated start reproduces the observed dynamic with a starting deficit
that the conventional start cannot carry.} The observed accumulation and its levelling-off are
recovered by all six models at the level, and bracketed by them in rate. \textbf{[corrected
2026-09-16]} A balanced start can also rise and level off, but only by attributing the whole of it to
litter changes inside the window; the two starts differ in the product $J\times\tau$ they imply, and
that difference is the headroom of the fourth conclusion.

\paragraph{Reproducing it requires more litter input, and faster turnover, than the published
parameterisations carry.} These are one displacement along a trade-off the data cannot resolve, and
the most plausible reading is not that the models are wrong but that the input flux is incomplete ---
with belowground and non-tree sources the candidates.

\paragraph{Where the calibration lands on that trade-off reaches the temperature sensitivity, and
through it the projection, but only for some structures.} A single climate modifier ties turnover to
temperature sensitivity, so the starting-state assumption that sets the position on the trade-off sets
the warming response with it; pool-specific modifiers do not. Models of the second kind are more robust to an assumption that cannot currently be
tested.

\paragraph{There is headroom, and taking it would cost something.} Five of the six models put
today's soil $28$--$49\%$ below the stock its present litter input would eventually support, which is
where Liski et al.\ (2006) also put it. That is space for further accumulation --- but realising it
means holding or raising the carbon actually returned to the soil, over decades, with the management
and economic consequences that implies. \textbf{And it postpones saturation rather than removing it:}
the same models that say there is room also say the room is finite.
\begin{ann}
\textbf{Annotation (p7):} ``\emph{there would be space for some more accumulation? But we need to
manage forests in a way that more C is returned to the soil, with all its economic consequences \dots\
(and it would be just postponing it, it will never go on indefinitely).}'' \textbf{Adopted as a
conclusion, with the number attached.} This is also the honest limit of the policy reading: a finite
ceiling is not a permanent sink.
\end{ann}

\paragraph{And a large, recoverable part of the plot-scale variance is invisible to every model
here.} It is a well-posed question for the next study rather than a limitation of this one.

\paragraph{Bonus product, not conclusion: the maps.} The calibrated ensemble yields gridded SOC and
its uncertainty for Finland, which is useful to the inventory and is offered as a deliverable rather
than as the paper's finding. \FIG{ENSEMBLE_SOC_mean}
