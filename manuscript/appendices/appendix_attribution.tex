\section{Where the modelled sink comes from: the inherited state, the litter change and the climate}
\label{app:attribution}

\subsection{The question and the answer in brief}

Each calibrated model produces a soil carbon sink that rises after 1985 and then slows.
The present appendix asks, for each year of 1985--2024 and for each model, how much of
that sink comes from three sources:
\begin{description}
  \item[The inherited state] the soil enters 1985 carrying a deficit: in the transient
    initialisation (Section~\ref{sec:init}) the litter rose before 1985, the soil lagged
    behind it, and in 1985 it holds less carbon than the litter of 1985 could sustain.
    Part of the sink after 1985 is the soil still catching up. The part is shown in dark
    green and labelled \emph{litter rise before 1985 (initialisation)}, because the deficit
    is itself the delayed effect of the litter history.
  \item[The litter change since 1985] the litter entering the soil departs, after 1985,
    from its 1985--1989 level (it rose by about half until 2006 and fell somewhat after).
    The 1985--1989 level is the one at which the transient initialisation ends its
    pre-run: the mean of the first five years of the litter series, used instead of 1985
    alone to smooth the noise of a single year and because the litter of 1985 is itself
    reconstructed from the 1986--1990 trend. The part is shown in light green.
  \item[The climate] the climate of each year departs from the reference climate
    (currently the 1985--2004 mean, the climate the initialisation also uses), speeding
    decomposition in warm years and slowing it in cold ones. All of a model's climate
    modifiers are moved together, so the three modifiers of Yasso15 and Yasso20 give one
    part, comparable with the single modifier of the other models. The part is shown in
    red.
\end{description}
The three parts add up to the model's sink in every year. They are obtained from three
simulations of the same model, removing one source at a time: the inherited state is what
disappears when the soil starts without a deficit, the climate is what disappears when, in
addition, the climate stops changing, and the litter change is what remains.

\subsection{The three runs}
\label{app:attr_runs}

For each plot and each posterior draw, the same calibrated model, with the same
parameters, is run three times from 1985:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{clll}
\hline
run & starting state in 1985 & litter & climate \\
\hline
1 & calibrated (with the inherited deficit) & observed & observed \\
2 & balanced (no deficit) & observed & observed \\
3 & balanced (no deficit) & observed & held at reference \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{description}
  \item[Run 1, the model as calibrated] the soil as the model actually simulates it: it
    starts from the end of the transient initialisation, carrying the deficit left by the
    litter rise before 1985, and receives the observed litter and climate. Its sink is the
    sink being divided, the one in every result of the paper.
  \item[Run 2, without a history] the same litter and climate, but the soil starts in
    balance: with the carbon the model would hold in 1985 had the litter always been at its
    1985--1989 level, under the reference climate. Decomposition then equals the litter
    entering, and there is no deficit to make up. The balanced start is the one the
    conventional steady-state assumption would give the same model; nothing is refitted to
    obtain it, and runs 2 and 3 are devices of the division only, never compared with
    observations.
  \item[Run 3, without a history and without climate change] as run 2, but the climate is
    held at its reference in every year. Only the litter still changes.
\end{description}

Each part of the sink is what disappears from one run to the next, with each run number
standing for that run's sink in a given year:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lp{5.3cm}p{5.0cm}}
\hline
part & the two runs differ only in & what it measures \\
\hline
inherited state $= 1-2$ & the starting state (litter and climate both observed) & what the deficit inherited from before 1985 adds to the sink \\
climate $= 2-3$ & the climate (both start balanced, both receive the observed litter) & what the observed climate adds, acting on the litter that actually arrived \\
litter change $= 3$ & --- & the sink of a balanced soil in an unchanging climate, which exists only because the litter departs from its 1985--1989 level \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The three parts add up to the sink of run 1 because the runs cancel in pairs,
$(1-2) + (2-3) + 3 = 1$. The adding-up is therefore arithmetic, not a finding; what gives
each part its meaning is explained next.

\subsection{Why the runs isolate the three sources}

All six models advance the soil one year at a time in the same way: in each year a fixed
share of the carbon in each pool decomposes or moves to another pool, and the litter of
the year is added; the shares are set by the model's parameters and by that year's climate.
Two consequences of this structure carry the division.

\paragraph{The starting carbon and the litter do not interact.} Because each pool loses a
fixed share of whatever it contains, the carbon that remains from the starting state and
the carbon added by the litter evolve independently, and the stock in any year is simply
their sum. Runs 1 and 2 receive the same litter under the same climate, so the carbon the
litter adds is the same in both; it disappears from their difference, which leaves only the
difference between the two starts, the deficit, closing year by year at the soil's own
pace. The inherited part, run 1 minus run 2, is the yearly closing of that gap. In a single
pool with a deficit of 10\,t\,C\,ha$^{-1}$ decomposing at 4\,\% per year, about
0.4\,t\,C\,ha$^{-1}$ of the gap closes in the first year and slightly less in each year
after. The litter after 1985 plays no role in the inherited part at all.

\paragraph{A balanced soil under unchanging conditions has no sink.} A soil in balance
loses each year exactly what it receives: a litter input of 4\,t\,C\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$
decomposing at 6\,\% per year balances at about 67\,t\,C\,ha$^{-1}$, and stays there as long
as the litter and the climate do not change. The balanced soil of runs 2 and 3 is built to be
in balance with the 1985--1989 litter under the reference climate, so in run 3, where the
climate stays at the reference, any sink can come only from the litter departing from its
1985--1989 level. Run 3 is therefore the litter part as it stands, and run 2 minus run 3,
where only the climate differs, is the climate part.

\paragraph{The climate does not combine additively.} The climate changes the shares that
decompose, so its effect depends on how much carbon is present: it acts on the extra litter,
and it acts on the inherited deficit. Neither joint effect can be separated from the parts by
the runs; each is assigned by the order in which the sources are removed:
\begin{description}
  \item[Climate and litter] the extra litter decomposes faster in warm years. The climate is
    removed after the litter has been allowed to change, so the joint effect is counted in
    the climate part, which therefore describes the climate acting on the litter that
    actually arrived. Over 1985--2024 the joint effect amounts to $-0.9$\,t\,C\,ha$^{-1}$ in
    the six-model average ($-0.5$ to $-1.3$ across models), against $+8.6$ for the litter
    part and $-7.5$ for the climate part.
  \item[Climate and the inherited state] the deficit closes faster in warm years. The
    inherited part is measured under the observed climate, so this joint effect is counted
    in the inherited part. Over 1985--2024 it amounts to $+0.7$\,t\,C\,ha$^{-1}$ in the
    six-model average, against $+9.3$ for the inherited part: about 7\,\%, from 3\,\% in SP1
    and Yasso20 to 10\,\% in TP2 and TP3, whose slow pools close the deficit over the
    longest time and so experience more of the warming.
\end{description}
Both joint effects are of the order of a tenth of the parts they sit in, and assigning them
otherwise would change no part's sign or its size relative to the others. The division is
therefore exact in its sum and approximate in the attribution of these two joint effects;
the approximation is stated rather than removed, because no assignment of a joint effect is
more correct than another.

\subsection{Computation and figures}

The runs use each model's own code and the plot's own litter and climate series, with no
approximation of the model. The division is computed for every plot of the set observed in
all three campaigns (310 plots, the basis of every observed rate in the paper) and for 50
posterior draws of each model. National values are plain averages over plots and draws, and
the six-model summary is the plain average of the six models; averages, unlike medians,
keep the parts adding up to the sink.

The figures show 1985--2024, the period simulated with observed forcing and the one on which
every part is anchored. Before 1985 the whole modelled sink is the soil catching up with the
litter rise, reported only as a total; after 2024 the projection holds the forcing constant,
and its parts would describe the soil settling, not the history the division explains. The
annual sink follows the weather of each year closely, so the figures are given both on
annual values and on a ten-year centred running mean; the same averaging is applied to every
part, so the smoothed parts still add up to the smoothed sink.

\subsection{Checks}

Three checks are run for every model, plot and posterior draw before any number is kept:
the pre-run replayed with the model's own run function reproduces the calibrated starting
state (largest relative difference $1.4\times10^{-14}$); the balanced soil with litter and
climate both held at their references keeps a sink of zero (largest departure
$2.3\times10^{-13}$\,t\,C\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$); and the three parts add up to the sink of
run 1 (within $10^{-15}$). Two further runs are made only to measure the two joint effects
reported above.

\subsection{Relation to a differential decomposition}

A simpler route divides the change in the sink from one year to the next, rather than the
sink itself, into the change in litter, the change in decomposition caused by the climate,
and the change in decomposition caused by the change in stock (the stock feedback). Each
term follows directly from the series of litter, climate and stock, with no further
simulation. The route answers a different question and has no part for the inherited state.
The stock feedback responds to the stock change of each year, which already contains
everything that has acted on the soil since the start, so the inherited deficit cannot be
separated from it; even when the deficit is the only driver, the term measures only how fast
the inherited sink fades, while the inherited sink itself sits in the starting value of the
sink, outside the terms. The division used here gives the inherited state a part of its own,
positive and directly comparable with the litter and the climate.

\subsection{What the division does and does not say}

The division describes the calibrated models, not the soil: each part is the share of the
modelled sink produced by one source, given the parameters retained by the calibration. The
inherited part is as large as the deficit the calibration chose, through
$\sigma_{\mathrm{init}}$, and it fades at the pace of each model's slow pools, so its size and
persistence differ between models for structural reasons; a model whose
$\sigma_{\mathrm{init}}$ is close to one carries almost no inherited sink.

The climate part depends on the reference climate. In the current runs the reference is the
1985--2004 mean, which the initialisation also applies to the whole pre-run, 1917--1984. The
modelled soil therefore enters 1985 already adjusted to a climate warmer than the decades
before it (plot means 2.1\,\textdegree C in 1961--1984 against 2.7\,\textdegree C in
1985--2004), and the climate part, measured against a mean of years that include the
observed period, sums to nearly zero until about 2004. The \emph{change} in the climate part
between periods is robust: its step between 1985--2004 and 2005--2024 follows the
1.2\,\textdegree C warming between the two. The planned correction is to use one constant
long-term mean of the climate before 1985 (from 1961, or from 1917 with a reconstruction)
both for the pre-run and as the reference: the soil then enters 1985 adjusted to the climate
it had lived in, and the climate part shows the whole warming since 1985. The correction
alters every model's initial state and requires a new calibration.

The litter product includes harvest residues and has no separate natural-mortality term, so
neither can be separated from the litter parts.

\subsection{Open choices for review}
\label{app:attribution_open}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The window of the long-term climate mean} for the pre-run and the reference:
    1961--1984 from the gridded observations already extracted per plot, or 1917--1984 with a
    reconstruction of 1917--1960.
  \item \textbf{The two joint effects with the climate}, assigned by the order of the runs
    (litter--climate to the climate part, inheritance--climate to the inherited part). Each
    is about a tenth of the part it sits in; reporting them separately, as two thin bands,
    is the alternative.
  \item \textbf{Annual or smoothed values} in the figures that enter the paper, and the width
    of the running mean.
  \item \textbf{The cumulative view} (carbon gained since 1985 by each part, S17) is held
    back: summed over time, the climate part reads as a growing effect of warming, and
    whether it adds to the annual view (S15, S16) is to be decided.
  \item \textbf{The differential decomposition} can be added as a complement, answering what
    changed the sink from one year to the next.
\end{enumerate}
