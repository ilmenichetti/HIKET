\section{Where the modelled sink comes from: the inherited state, the litter change and the climate}
\label{app:attribution}

\subsection{The question and the answer in brief}

Each calibrated model produces a soil carbon sink that rises after 1985 and then slows.
The present appendix asks, for each year of 1985--2024 and for each model, how much of
that sink comes from three sources:
\begin{description}
  \item[The inherited state] the soil enters 1985 carrying a deficit: in the transient
    initialisation (Section~\ref{sec:init}) the litter rose before 1985, the soil lagged
    behind it, and in 1985 it holds less carbon than the litter of 1985 could sustain.
    Part of the sink after 1985 is the soil still catching up. The part is shown in dark
    green and labelled \emph{litter rise before 1985 (initialisation)}, because the deficit
    is itself the delayed effect of the litter history.
  \item[The litter change since 1985] the litter entering the soil departs, after 1985,
    from its 1985--1989 level (it rose by about half until 2006 and fell somewhat after).
    The 1985--1989 level is the one at which the transient initialisation ends its
    pre-run: the mean of the first five years of the litter series, used instead of 1985
    alone to smooth the noise of a single year and because the litter of 1985 is itself
    reconstructed from the 1986--1990 trend. The part is shown in light green.
  \item[The climate] the climate of each year departs from the reference climate
    (the 1961--1984 mean, the climate the initialisation also uses), speeding
    decomposition in warm years and slowing it in cold ones. All of a model's climate
    modifiers are moved together, so the three modifiers of Yasso15 and Yasso20 give one
    part, comparable with the single modifier of the other models. The part is shown in
    red.
\end{description}
The answer comes from three calibrations of each model, fitted to the same measurements
under three assumptions that remove one source at a time. The inherited state is what the
sink loses when the soil is assumed to start in balance; the climate is what it loses when,
in addition, the climate is assumed not to change; the litter change is what remains. Over
1985--2024 the six models gain $+8.0$\,t\,C\,ha$^{-1}$ on average: $+11.6$ inherited, $+7.3$
from the litter change and $-11.0$ from the climate. The inherited state carries more than
the whole sink in every model.

\subsection{Three calibrations}
\label{app:attr_runs}

Each model is calibrated three times, each time with one more assumption, and each time on
the same measurements, with the same priors, error model and sampling (a balanced start
fixes $\sigma_{\mathrm{init}}$ at one, so that parameter is not estimated in runs 2 and 3):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{clll}
\hline
run & starting state in 1985 & litter & climate after 1985 \\
\hline
1 & estimated (with the inherited deficit) & observed & observed \\
2 & balanced (no deficit) & observed & observed \\
3 & balanced (no deficit) & observed & held at reference \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{description}
  \item[Run 1, the model of the paper] the calibration used in every other result: the
    starting state is estimated through the transient initialisation, so the soil carries
    the deficit left by the litter rise before 1985, and receives the observed litter and
    climate. Its sink is the sink being divided.
  \item[Run 2, a balanced start] the same, except that the soil is assumed to start in
    balance: with the carbon the model would hold in 1985 had the litter always been at its
    1985--1989 level, under the reference climate. The balanced start is the one the
    conventional steady-state assumption gives; the calibration is the balanced-start
    comparison of the main text.
  \item[Run 3, a balanced start and an unchanging climate] as run 2, but the climate is held
    at its reference in every year after 1985, in the calibration as in the simulation. Only
    the litter still changes.
\end{description}

Each part of the sink is the difference between two runs, with each run number standing for
that run's sink in a given year:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lp{5.3cm}p{5.0cm}}
\hline
part & the two runs differ in the assumption & what it measures \\
\hline
inherited state $= 1-2$ & of the starting state & what the deficit inherited from before 1985 adds to the sink \\
climate $= 2-3$ & of the climate after 1985 & what the observed climate adds, acting on the litter that actually arrived \\
litter change $= 3$ & --- & the sink of a balanced soil in an unchanging climate, which exists only because the litter departs from its 1985--1989 level \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The three parts add up to the sink of run 1 because the runs cancel in pairs,
$(1-2) + (2-3) + 3 = 1$. The adding-up is therefore arithmetic, not a finding; what gives
each part its meaning is explained next.

\subsection{What each part means}

\paragraph{A comparison between assumptions.} Every run is a model fitted to the
measurements, so each part compares two fitted models that differ by one assumption. When
the soil is assumed to start in balance, the calibration does not only remove the deficit:
it also moves the parameters to explain the measured stocks without one (in most models
less litter input, and faster, more temperature-sensitive decomposition; main text). The
inherited part includes that response, and so does the climate part. Each part is therefore
the change in the modelled sink that follows from one assumption, once the data have been
allowed to answer it, and not the effect of one source on a model held fixed. The same
logic as the balanced-start comparison of the main text applies throughout, and no part
borrows parameters estimated under a different assumption.

\paragraph{A balanced soil under unchanging conditions has no sink.} A soil in balance
loses each year exactly what it receives: a litter input of 4\,t\,C\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$
decomposing at 6\,\% per year balances at about 67\,t\,C\,ha$^{-1}$, and stays there as long
as the litter and the climate do not change. The balanced soil of run 3 is in balance with
the 1985--1989 litter under the reference climate, and the climate stays at the reference,
so any sink of run 3 can come only from the litter departing from its 1985--1989 level.
The property holds for any parameter values, and so for the run-3 calibration as for any
other: run 3 is the litter part as it stands.

\paragraph{The joint effects are inside the parts.} The climate changes the share of carbon
that decomposes each year, so its effect depends on how much carbon is present: it acts on
the extra litter, and it acts on the inherited deficit. Each of the two therefore has a joint
effect with the climate, an amount of sink that exists only when both are present, and that
belongs to neither source alone (asking how much of it is due to each is like asking how much
of the area of a rectangle is due to its width and how much to its height). In the three
parts the joint effects are assigned by the order of the runs: the inherited part is measured
under the observed climate and so contains the joint effect of climate and inheritance; the
climate part is measured on the observed litter and so contains the joint effect of climate
and litter. Reporting the two joint effects as terms of their own requires two further
calibrations, with the inherited deficit and the climate held at the reference, and with a
balanced start and the litter held at its 1985--1989 level; both are planned.

\subsection{Computation and figures}

The runs use each model's own code and the plot's own litter and climate series, with no
approximation of the model. The division is computed for every plot of the set observed in
all three campaigns (310 plots, the basis of every observed rate in the paper) and for 50
posterior draws of each calibration. National values are plain averages over plots and
draws, and the six-model summary is the plain average of the six models; averages, unlike
medians, keep the parts adding up to the sink.

The figures show 1985--2024, the period simulated with observed forcing and the one on which
every part is anchored. Before 1985 the whole modelled sink is the soil catching up with the
litter rise, reported only as a total; after 2024 the projection holds the forcing constant,
and its parts would describe the soil settling, not the history the division explains. The
annual sink follows the weather of each year closely, so the figures are given both on
annual values and on a ten-year centred running mean; the same averaging is applied to every
part, so the smoothed parts still add up to the smoothed sink.

\subsection{Checks}
\label{app:attr_checks}

The three calibrations of each model converged (largest potential scale reduction factor
1.009, effective sample size above 1700 for every parameter). Two checks are run for every
model before any number is kept. For every draw of the two balanced-start calibrations, the
balanced soil with the litter and the climate both held at their references keeps a sink of
zero on every plot (largest departure $1.7\times10^{-13}$\,t\,C\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$), so the
balanced start is in balance under each calibration's own parameters. The three parts add up
to the sink of run 1 in every year and draw (within $10^{-15}$).

The litter and climate parts are narrow, because they follow the measured litter and climate.
The inherited part is the uncertain one: it is set by the size of the deficit, which the
calibration estimates through $\sigma_{\mathrm{init}}$, and it carries most of the uncertainty of
the sink. The litter change and the climate are of the same order and of opposite sign, and
together they remove a little carbon in every model, from $-1.0$ (Yasso15) to
$-5.3$\,t\,C\,ha$^{-1}$ (TP3); their sum is the sink of run 2, and the balanced-start calibration
loses carbon over 1985--2024 in every one of its draws. The inherited part therefore exceeds the
whole sink in every model. SP1 stands apart, with the largest litter and climate parts and the
smallest sink: its single pool receives all the litter and responds to the climate as a whole,
where the other models pass part of the carbon to slower pools.

\subsection{Relation to a differential decomposition}

A simpler route divides the change in the sink from one year to the next, rather than the
sink itself, into the change in litter, the change in decomposition caused by the climate,
and the change in decomposition caused by the change in stock (the stock feedback). Each
term follows directly from the series of litter, climate and stock, with no further
simulation. The route answers a different question and has no part for the inherited state.
The stock feedback responds to the stock change of each year, which already contains
everything that has acted on the soil since the start, so the inherited deficit cannot be
separated from it; even when the deficit is the only driver, the term measures only how fast
the inherited sink fades, while the inherited sink itself sits in the starting value of the
sink, outside the terms. The division used here gives the inherited state a part of its own,
positive and directly comparable with the litter and the climate.

\subsection{What the division does and does not say}

The division describes the calibrated models, not the soil: each part is the share of the
modelled sink that follows from one assumption, given what the calibrations retain under it.
The inherited part is as large as the deficit the calibration chose, and it fades at the pace
of each model's slow pools, so its size and persistence differ between models for structural
reasons.

The climate part depends on the reference climate. The reference is one constant long-term
mean of the 1961--1984 climate (plot mean 2.2\,\textdegree C), from the same gridded
observations, used both for the pre-run 1917--1984 and as the reference: the soil enters 1985
adjusted to the climate it had lived in, and the climate part shows the whole warming since
then. A reference that overlaps the observed period would hide part of that warming and
shrink the climate part. The \emph{size} of the climate part therefore rests on the choice of
the reference, whereas its change between periods, and the ordering of the parts, do not.

The litter product includes harvest residues and has no separate natural-mortality term, so
neither can be separated from the litter parts.

\subsection{Open choices for review}
\label{app:attribution_open}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Three parts or five terms}: the joint effects assigned by the order of the runs
    (as now), or reported as their own two terms once the two further calibrations exist
    (planned with the new input data).
  \item \textbf{Annual or smoothed values} in the figures that enter the paper, and the width
    of the running mean.
  \item \textbf{The cumulative view} (carbon gained since 1985 by each part, S17) is held
    back: summed over time, the climate part reads as a growing effect of warming, and
    whether it adds to the annual view (S15, S16) is to be decided.
  \item \textbf{The differential decomposition} can be added as a complement, answering what
    changed the sink from one year to the next.
\end{enumerate}
