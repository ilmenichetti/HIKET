\section{Posterior distribution of RMSE}
\label{app:rmse-posterior}

The multimodel metrics table reports a single RMSE per model, computed from the
posterior-mean prediction. That number says nothing about how tightly the fit is
pinned: two calibrations with the same central RMSE can disagree with themselves
by very different amounts. Here RMSE is recomputed for \emph{every} posterior
draw, so each panel of Figure~\ref{fig:rmse-posterior} is a distribution whose
location is the fit and whose width is the posterior's own disagreement about it.
Calibration and held-out plots are drawn together, because the gap between them
is the part that is not merely a fit statistic.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/S14_rmse_posterior.png}
  \caption{Posterior distribution of RMSE, one panel per model, recomputed for each
  of the 100 posterior draws. Blue = calibration plots, red = held-out plots;
  dashed lines are medians; densities are scaled to a common height so shape is
  comparable across panels. The horizontal range is held fixed across runs so that
  successive calibrations can be laid side by side.}
  \label{fig:rmse-posterior}
\end{figure}

Two features are worth noting. First, the six models are barely separated in
central RMSE (calibration medians span $43.8$--$45.1$~tC\,ha$^{-1}$), which is the
same message the skill metrics carry: structure buys very little here. Second, and
less visible in a point estimate, the calibration--holdout gap is \emph{not}
uniform across the ensemble, and it separates the ensemble cleanly in the wrong
direction. The three models with the \emph{best} calibration RMSE --- SP1, TP2 and
TP3 --- carry the three \emph{largest} gaps ($+1.4$, $+3.1$, $+3.1$~tC\,ha$^{-1}$),
while the three that fit the calibration set least well close theirs or reverse it:
Yasso20 $+0.3$, Yasso07 $+1.0$, and Yasso15 does slightly \emph{better} on held-out
plots than on calibration ones ($45.0 \rightarrow 44.5$). The models that fit best
are therefore the ones that generalise worst. This is not explained by the size of
the input multiplier, as one might expect: $\sigma_{\text{input}}$ is largest in
Yasso07 ($2.10$), which has one of the smallest gaps, and across the ensemble the
correlation between multiplier and gap is weakly negative ($-0.25$).

This figure is intended as a comparison instrument across calibrations. Where a
prior is tightened onto a physical referent, the expectation is that fit degrades;
the quantity of interest is then not only how far the distribution moves but
whether its width moves with it, since a constraint that shifts the location while
leaving the spread untouched is doing something different from one that does both.

That comparison has now been made once. Tightening the auxiliary-parameter priors
(the widths of $\sigma_{\text{init}}$ and $\sigma_{\text{input}}$, and the centre of
the latter) degraded calibration RMSE in five of the six models, by $+0.25$ to
$+1.06$~tC\,ha$^{-1}$, while held-out RMSE moved by at most $+0.28$. The
calibration--holdout gap consequently narrowed in \emph{every} model, in one case
changing sign. The cost of the constraint was therefore paid almost entirely in fit
that was not generalising, which is the outcome a physically-motivated prior is
supposed to produce and is not visible in $R^{2}$, which barely moved.
